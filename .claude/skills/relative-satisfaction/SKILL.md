# 相对满足/错反应倾向 (Relative Satisfaction)

## Overview

Relative Satisfaction refers to how human satisfaction often depends on relative comparison rather than absolute values. We tend to compare now with the past, or with others, thereby deciding happiness or失落. This leads to behavior pursuing relative advantage rather than absolute improvement, sometimes causing misjudgment of action opportunities. Psychological research shows that happiness largely comes from comparison with one's own past and those around. For example, if income increases but not as much as others' increases, there is still dissatisfaction. This explains why when the economy develops but relative gaps widen, social dissatisfaction remains strong, and is also the root of envy and comparison. Managers can use positive relative incentives (like leaderboard competitions), but should also guard against negatives (cooperation becoming involution). Additionally, relative thinking makes us focus too much on comparison in decisions and ignore absolute benefits, like being willing to self-harm to beat opponents. Munger warns not to make foolish risks due to comparison.

## When to Apply

- Personal finance: Try to measure with absolute goals, such as rationally assessing whether a purchase is worthwhile, rather than indulging in "could have earned more" type relative comparisons
- Employee incentives: Can use ranking rewards to boost motivation, but note that too large gaps harm team collaboration and balance; also emphasize the importance of achieving absolute goals
- View on happiness: Cultivate mindset of "not comparing with others, only comparing with self," reduce unnecessary comparison dissatisfaction, focus more on absolute progress (better than last year is good)

## How to Use

1. Set absolute goals rather than purely relative benchmarks
2. Focus on your own progress rather than others' achievements
3. Recognize that happiness is relative but can be cultivated through absolute focus
4. Design incentive systems that balance relative and absolute rewards
5. Accept that comparison will always exist but can be managed

## Examples

### Example 1: Year-End Bonus
Someone receives a year-end bonus of $10,000 and is satisfied, but upon hearing a colleague got $20,000, immediately feels cheated—even though $10,000 is actually quite good absolutely.

### Example 2: Discount Shopping
Consumers are prone to impulsive purchases during discount seasons, because the $100 original price now $50 feels like a gain (relatively cheap), but may not need the product at all (absolutely spent $50).

### Example 3: Stock Selling
In stock trading, some don't want to sell winning stocks because they've fallen from their highest point, falling into the relative comparison of "not selling at the highest is a loss," missing opportunities to cash in profits.

## Core Principles

- Happiness is comparative - it depends on reference points
- Relative comparison can distort absolute value assessment
- Comparison with self is healthier than comparison with others
- Incentive systems can leverage or counteract relative thinking
- Absolute gains matter more than relative wins

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Relative comparison leads to perpetual dissatisfaction
- Warning: Competition can become destructive internal comparison
- Mistake: Making decisions based on what could have been rather than what is
- Mistake: Ignoring absolute gains in pursuit of relative advantage
- Benefit: Absolute focus leads to greater contentment
- Benefit: Relative incentives can motivate performance
