# 易得性偏误 (Availability Heuristic)

## Overview

Availability Heuristic refers to the tendency for people to judge event probability or importance based on the most easily recalled information in memory. This causes recent, prominent, and emotionally strong examples to have excessive influence on judgments, while statistical objective facts are ignored. This shows that our intuitive probability assessments are very unreliable, often distorted by news bombardment and personal experiences. For example, frequent airplane crash reports cause people to overestimate air crash risk, though statistically driving is more dangerous. Investors may also become overly optimistic after just experiencing a bull market. Munger warns that this bias leads to decision deviation and should be corrected with data and rationality. This bias can also be utilized: for example, making correct concepts appear frequently in teams to strengthen impressions and influence decision tendencies.

## When to Apply

- Risk assessment: Don't rely only on recent news or personal experiences; look at long-term large-sample data
- Management: If there are important but easily forgotten risks, periodically remind (create availability) to get employees to pay attention, such as quarterly safety training reviews
- Communication: When persuading others, provide more vivid examples, because examples make the viewpoint more available in the other person's mind and more convincing

## How to Use

1. Seek statistical data rather than relying on memorable examples
2. Be aware that recent events are more available in memory
3. Actively seek out information about risks that don't make the news
4. Use repetition to make important information more available
5. Question whether your risk assessment is based on data or memorability

## Examples

### Example 1: Shark Attacks vs. Coconut Deaths
People often think dying from shark attacks is more likely than being killed by falling coconuts, because shark attacks are more common in media coverage and memorable, but actually more people die from coconuts each year.

### Example 2: Safety Incidents
After a company experiences a safety incident, management will be highly nervous and invest lots of resources in rectification for the next few months, but after years of calm, vigilance decreases. This is due to recent memory dominating judgment.

## Core Principles

- Intuitive probability assessments are unreliable
- News distorts our sense of actual risk frequencies
- Recent and emotionally strong examples dominate judgment
- Statistical data should override memorable examples
- What is prominent is not necessarily what is important

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Media coverage creates distorted risk perceptions
- Warning: Recent experiences bias future predictions
- Mistake: Using memorable examples rather than statistics for decisions
- Mistake: Ignoring risks that don't make headlines
- Benefit: Awareness of this bias leads to more data-driven decisions
- Benefit: Repetition can be used ethically to reinforce important concepts
