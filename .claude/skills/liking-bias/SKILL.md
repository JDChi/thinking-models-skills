# 喜好/厌恶偏⻅ (Liking & Disliking Bias)

## Overview

Liking and disliking bias refers to the tendency for people to overestimate the advantages of people or things they like while underestimating their disadvantages, and do the opposite for those they dislike. That is, preconceived feelings distort our subsequent objective judgment. This bias shows that our judgments are often colored by emotions. If we like a person, we are more easily convinced by them and more easily forgive their faults; if we dislike a person, even if they have a point, we will refute them. In decision-making, this can lead to cronyism or rejection of beneficial information. Munger suggests using checklists and rational processes to partially counteract this bias. Also, it can be used in communication: first establish a friendly atmosphere so the other party likes you, then your views will be more easily accepted.

## When to Apply

- Recruitment: Interviews should be structured to avoid deciding on employment or elimination based solely on "gut feeling"; multiple interviewers can be introduced to balance individual likes and dislikes
- Cooperation and negotiation: First establish friendly relationships, such as small talk to find common ground, to increase the other party's good feelings toward you, making them more open to your positions in negotiations
- Self-reflection: Check whether you are overlooking hidden dangers because you like a certain plan, or rejecting useful opinions because you dislike a certain source; try to evaluate people and matters separately
- Important decisions: Ask yourself "Am I unconsciously favoring/biased because I like/dislike?"

## How to Use

1. Use structured evaluation criteria rather than relying on personal feelings
2. Separate your evaluation of the person from evaluation of their ideas
3. Seek input from people who don't know their personal feelings toward the subject
4. Use checklists to ensure objective criteria are considered
5. Be aware when personal feelings might be distorting your judgment

## Examples

### Example 1: Fan Blindness
"Fan filter" is a manifestation of liking bias. Fans overlook their idol's flaws and even beautify ordinary actions.

### Example 2: Manager Disliking an Employee
If a supervisor doesn't like a certain employee, they may unconsciously nitpick their work, even finding faults where none exist even when the work is done well.

### Example 3: Brand Loyalty and Bias
Consumer likes or dislikes for brands also affect evaluation: people who like the Apple brand tolerate its high prices and defects, while those who dislike it criticize even good products.

## Core Principles

- Preconceived emotions distort subsequent objective judgment
- Liking leads to easier persuasion and forgiveness of faults
- Disliking causes rejection of valid points
- Checklists and rational processes can partially counteract this bias
- Friendly atmosphere in communication increases receptiveness to views

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Personal liking can lead to cronyism in hiring and partnerships
- Warning: Disliking a source can cause rejection of valid information
- Mistake: Using "gut feeling" rather than objective criteria in evaluations
- Mistake: Confusing evaluation of a person with evaluation of their ideas
- Benefit: Understanding this bias helps create fair evaluation systems
- Benefit: Awareness allows using liking bias positively in legitimate persuasion
