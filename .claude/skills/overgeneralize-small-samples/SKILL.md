# 以偏概全 (Overgeneralize from Small Samples)

## Overview

Overgeneralize from Small Samples refers to the tendency for humans to use experiences from small samples to derive general laws, ignoring statistical significance. This is an oversight of the law of large numbers, and in psychology is also a combination of availability and representativeness biases. Overgeneralizing from small samples leads to many superstitions and wrong cognitions. For example, individuals encountering several special cases feel "it's always like this." Munger warns not to lightly trust single cases to draw big conclusions but to look at long-term large data. This tendency is very dangerous in business decisions, like changing strategy based on one or two customer feedbacks. Overcoming it requires discipline: when facing something, ask "Is my sample large enough? Is this conclusion robust?"

## When to Apply

- Analysis: When involving statistical inference, ensure the sample is large enough and representative; otherwise, say "insufficient data to conclude"
- Management: When listening to customer and employee feedback, don't change everything based on individual voices; look for majority trends
- Personal experience: Acknowledge that personal experience is limited; don't assume based on your own or a few people's experiences

## How to Use

1. Require statistically significant sample sizes before drawing conclusions
2. Seek out counterexamples that challenge your generalizations
3. Distinguish between correlation and causation
4. Accept that personal experience is limited and potentially biased
5. Look for large-scale data before making important decisions

## Examples

### Example 1: Stock Trading Success
Someone makes money on three consecutive short-term stock trades, concludes they are gifted and "stock market wealth is easy," then increases bets and loses big on the fourth trade—the first three were just random luck, and the small sample caused misjudgment of skill level.

### Example 2: Medical Treatment Claims
In medicine, if a new treatment works on 4 out of 5 patients and the doctor immediately declares a breakthrough, the sample is actually too small and might be coincidence; more trials are needed to confirm effectiveness.

## Core Principles

- Small samples can produce misleading patterns
- Law of large numbers requires large samples for reliable conclusions
- Personal experience is limited and potentially unrepresentative
- Statistical significance must be considered before generalizing
- Correlation does not imply causation

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Single anecdotes can create false confidence in generalizations
- Warning: Small samples can show patterns that disappear at scale
- Mistake: Changing strategy based on few data points
- Mistake: Assuming personal experience represents broader reality
- Benefit: Large-sample thinking leads to better decisions
- Benefit: Seeking counterexamples improves analysis quality
