# 承诺与一致性偏差 (Commitment & Consistency Bias)

## Overview

Commitment & Consistency Bias refers to the tendency for people to maintain consistency with their previous commitments and behaviors, even when those commitments later prove inappropriate. Due to image maintenance or psychological comfort, we often stubbornly adhere to original positions (even if wrong), unwilling to be seen as fickle. Consistency bias makes us "follow through," but also "refuse to admit error." Munger calls it "the foolish consistency of small minds." Many people know a decision is poor but don't want to withdraw due to prior commitment investment (related to sunk cost fallacy). Understanding this tendency helps design mechanisms in organizations for "letting go of the old and embracing the new," encouraging admitting errors and timely correction, so personal face doesn't obstruct rational adjustment. Also, in persuasion fields, using this bias can get people to make small commitments that eventually lead to large compliance (foot-in-the-door effect).

## When to Apply

- Decision correction: Organizational culture should allow decision modification, not viewing admitting mistakes as incompetence. Munger advocates "recognizing and correcting errors is worthy of reward"
- Negotiation and sales: Gradually get the other party to make small acknowledgments or commitments; for consistency, they will be more inclined toward subsequent large cooperation (phased contract strategy)
- Self-management: Be alert to whether you're not giving up wrong decisions due to stubbornness; dare to cut losses

## How to Use

1. Design systems that allow graceful revision of positions
2. Use small commitments strategically to build toward larger goals
3. Separate self-image from specific decisions
4. Recognize when consistency is irrational
5. Create cultures that reward admitting mistakes

## Examples

### Example 1: Politician's Failed Policy
A certain politician knows the policy failed but because they promised it during the election, they stubbornly persist in not changing, otherwise appearing inconsistent.

### Example 2: Escalating Commitment to Failing Projects
Many large projects start with wrong decisions, but after investing some resources, decision-makers "persist to the end," continuously adding resources, ultimately suffering greater losses (classic cases like the Vietnam War, US-Soviet space race moon political commitments).

### Example 3: Public Fitness Commitments
Ordinary people similarly, if they publicly set a flag to lose weight, even discovering during the process that the method is unhealthy, they stubbornly continue execution to avoid being laughed at for not keeping promises.

## Core Principles

- "Foolish consistency" prevents learning from mistakes
- Sunk cost fallacy is related to commitment consistency
- Small commitments can lead to larger compliance
- Organizations should allow graceful revision of positions
- Admitting errors should be rewarded, not punished

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Escalating commitment to failing projects
- Warning: Personal face obstructs rational decision-making
- Mistake: Continuing wrong decisions to appear consistent
- Mistake: Not separating self-image from specific commitments
- Benefit: Foot-in-door technique works for ethical persuasion
- Benefit: Follow-through on good commitments builds trust
