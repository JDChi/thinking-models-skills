# 代表性偏⻅ (Representativeness Heuristic)

## Overview

Representativeness Heuristic refers to the tendency for us to classify things based on their similarity to a typical model, ignoring statistical base rates and other information. This leads to errors like base rate neglect and stereotypes. Representativeness bias causes people to generalize based on partial information: seeing a few characteristics like a certain type of thing, they conclude it is that type, without considering the overall probability of that type appearing. Munger's example of the "Linda problem" shows how people choose options that seem more "representative" but are actually less likely, violating logic. Understanding this helps us add statistical thinking to judgments and avoid being deceived by surface stories. It also has significance for preventing discrimination, not sticking definitive labels on someone based on a few tags.

## When to Apply

- Recruitment: Don't be fooled by certain candidate traits thinking they must be suitable for a position; reference more objective tests and background verification
- Decision-making: When evaluating probability, first consider base rates, then combine with specific information for correction. For example, new product launch success rate is only 10% on average, even with experienced team, can't estimate 80% success based on feeling
- Avoiding bias: Don't judge people by appearances, don't stereotypically infer traits just because someone belongs to a certain group

## How to Use

1. Always consider base rates before making probability judgments
2. Question whether you're being swayed by stereotypes
3. Seek statistical evidence rather than relying on surface similarities
4. Be aware that representative stories feel true but may be statistically unlikely
5. Separate the evaluation of characteristics from the evaluation of overall probability

## Examples

### Example 1: Occupation Guess
If someone is described as "outgoing and helpful," many guess they are a salesperson or teacher rather than an engineer, even though engineers are more numerous overall. This is judging by stereotype and ignoring base rates.

### Example 2: Investor Seeing "Steve Jobs-Like" Founder
Investors seeing a startup founder who looks like Steve Jobs may arbitrarily believe the company will succeed, ignoring the statistical fact that startup success rates are extremely low.

## Core Principles

- Intuitive classification is unreliable
- Base rates matter more than surface characteristics
- Stereotypes cause systematic misjudgment
- Statistical thinking counteracts representativeness bias
- Stories feel true but may be statistically unlikely

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Stereotyping leads to discrimination and misjudgment
- Warning: "Linda problem" shows people violate logic for representativeness
- Mistake: Ignoring base rates in favor of good stories
- Mistake: Assuming characteristics of a group apply to individuals
- Benefit: Statistical thinking improves judgment accuracy
- Benefit: Awareness prevents unfair stereotyping
