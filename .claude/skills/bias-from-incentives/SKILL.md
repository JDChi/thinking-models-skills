# 激励偏差 (Bias from Incentives)

## Overview

Bias from incentives describes the phenomenon where, due to personal interests or incentives, our judgments unconsciously tilt toward directions that benefit ourselves. As the saying goes, "Benefits come first, reason follows," and even we ourselves may not realize this bias. This bias explains many seemingly inexplicable poor decisions or moral problems. Charlie Munger called it the number one cause of human misjudgment: "He who takes the bread is easy to bend." Whether experts or ordinary people, once interests are involved, it is difficult to remain objective. This reminds us to try to avoid direct personal interest inducements in decision-making, or design incentives so that interests align with the correct decision direction. Additionally, for others' suggestions or opinions, also consider what their interest motivations might be.

## When to Apply

- Management: When designing incentive mechanisms, be careful to avoid causing employees to harm long-term goals for short-term benefits
- Decision-making: When decision-makers listen to various opinions, consider the speaker's interest position
- Self-reflection: When making judgments involving personal interests, consciously examine whether you are objective, and seek independent opinions or data verification when necessary
- Evaluating advice: When receiving advice, consider the advisor's incentives and potential biases

## How to Use

1. Recognize that everyone has a "price" and is subject to incentive bias
2. Design incentive systems that align personal gain with organizational goals
3. When seeking advice, ask yourself what the advisor gains from their recommendation
4. Seek independent verification when important decisions involve your own interests
5. Use systems and rationality to counteract incentive bias

## Examples

### Example 1: Salesperson Believing in Their Product
Salespeople often sincerely believe their product is the best because commission incentives cause them to unconsciously overlook shortcomings and exaggerate strengths.

### Example 2: Doctor Prescribing Medications
Doctors who receive kickbacks from pharmaceutical companies may prescribe that company's medications more frequently, even when not the best choice.

### Example 3: Enron Scandal
Auditors who have been employed by a company for a long time may relax their review of financial reports. In the Enron incident, the accounting firm overlooked violations due to consulting fee interests, ultimately causing disaster.

## Core Principles

- "He who takes the bread is easy to bend" - incentives distort judgment
- Incentive bias is the number one cause of human misjudgment
- Everyone has a price, and systems should neutralize incentive bias
- Consider advisors' interests when evaluating their recommendations
- Align incentives with long-term goals, not just short-term performance

## Warning Signs / Common Mistakes / Benefits

- Warning: Sales commissions tied only to sales volume incentivize ignoring customer experience
- Warning: Long-term relationships with clients can lead to reduced objectivity
- Mistake: Assuming experts are objective without considering their incentives
- Mistake: Designing incentives that reward short-term gains at the expense of long-term health
- Benefit: Properly aligned incentives motivate desired behaviors
- Benefit: Awareness of bias helps you seek more objective input
